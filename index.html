How to read this page

FII, DII, Client, Pro
FII is foreign institutions, DII is domestic institutions (mutual funds, insurers), Client is retail and other individual traders, Pro is proprietary trading desks of brokers and arbitrage firms.
Long, Short, Net
Long means contracts bought, a bet on a rise. Short means contracts sold, a bet on a fall. Net is long minus short. A positive net means the group is net long.
Index vs stock segments
Index futures and options track Nifty or Bank Nifty. Stock futures and options track single shares. This data is by category, not by stock name.
Open interest (OI)
Number of contracts still open. Rising OI with a rising price usually means fresh buying; rising OI with a falling price usually means fresh selling.
Put-call ratio (PCR)
Put OI divided by call OI. Above 1 is often read as bullish support, below 0.7 as bearish. Extreme readings can also signal crowded positioning.
Max pain
The strike where option buyers collectively lose the most. Prices often drift toward it near expiry.
OI walls
For the nearest expiry: the strike at or below spot with the largest put OI (a likely floor) and the strike at or above spot with the largest call OI (a likely ceiling).
SMA and EMA
Moving averages of closing price over N days. SMA is the plain average; EMA weights recent days more. Price above them suggests an uptrend.
India VIX and implied move
India VIX is the annualised volatility the options market expects. Spot times VIX times the square root of (days / 365) gives a one-standard-deviation expected move for the period; roughly two-thirds of outcomes fall inside it.
ATR
Average True Range over 14 days: the typical daily price swing in points. It shows volatility, not direction.
Option spreads
A spread combines a bought and a sold option so the maximum loss is capped. Examples: bull put spread, bear call spread, iron condor.

Foreign flows: FIIs were net buyers of about Rs 10,308 crore. Foreign buying is the most reliable fuel for broad rallies, because it tends to go into the index heavyweights (private banks, IT, large caps), which have the most weight in Nifty and Sensex. It also supports the rupee, which lowers imported-inflation worries and invites further foreign money. Domestic flows: DIIs were net sellers of about Rs 4,860 crore. Domestic funds sell mostly to book profits or meet redemptions. When they sell into a foreign-led rally it often signals a stretched market; when they sell while foreigners also sell, nobody is absorbing the supply and falls can extend.

Net picture: foreigners are buying while domestic funds sell. This is common near short-term highs, when domestic funds book profits. The rally is then dependent on foreign money, which can reverse quickly if global cues turn.

Foreigners are cautious but not extreme, so the market can still rise, though rallies tend to meet selling from this group. Versus the previous day the net moved by -2,207 contracts toward short, through fresh shorts or long unwinding, a mildly negative change. Index futures are the cleanest gauge of directional conviction because they carry no option premium, which is why this line is tracked more closely than any other.

FII index options: net call position -109,607 and net put position +384,732 contracts. This reads as net long puts and net short calls, a bearish combination (positioned for a fall or protecting against one). Compare it with the futures stance above: when options and futures agree, conviction is higher; when they disagree (for example short futures but short puts), the group is often hedging a book or running a spread rather than expressing one view.

FII stock futures: net +1,001,620 contracts (long 3,673,303, short 2,671,683), a change of +10,069 on the day. Stock futures show conviction in individual shares rather than the index. Rising stock-future longs alongside a rising index suggest broad participation, which makes a rally healthier; a rising index with falling stock-future longs suggests a narrow, heavyweight-led move that is easier to reverse.

DII derivatives: index futures net +17,151 contracts (change -5,615). Stock futures net -3,892,321 contracts. Domestic institutions mostly use derivatives to hedge large equity portfolios, so a net short futures position is usually a hedge against their cash holdings and not a bearish call. The useful signal is the change: when DIIs add to hedges it often means they expect near-term weakness, and when they lift hedges they are comfortable holding risk. Because their cash-market buying is steady, they rarely drive the index direction through derivatives.

Client index futures: long 155,681, short 114,419, net +41,262 contracts (long share 58%, previous day 59%). The Client category is mainly retail and high-net-worth traders. It is a useful crowd gauge because this group is often positioned against institutions near turning points. Right now clients and FIIs are on opposite sides in index futures. Institutions are generally the better-capitalised and better-informed side, so a large gap means the market is leaning on the institutional view; the risk is that if the crowd side is wrong, it unwinds together and adds momentum to the move.

Client index options: net call -34,321 and net put -488,506 contracts, reading as net short both calls and puts, which is typical of premium selling: the group expects the market to stay inside a range and is paid for time decay, but loses if a sharp move arrives. Retail option buyers lose money more often than they win, so a heavy net-buyer stance means premium sellers (mainly Pro and FII) are on the other side; as expiry approaches, time decay works for the sellers.

Pro (proprietary desks): index futures net +12,041 contracts (change +17,584). In index options they are net long both calls and puts, which is typical of volatility buying: the group is paying for protection or expects a large move in either direction. Pro desks run arbitrage, market-making and hedged option books, so their net number is largely a balancing item that offsets the other groups. Large daily changes usually reflect expiry-related adjustments or hedging of option books, and are best read together with the option open interest below, not as a directional view.

Who is on which side (index futures): net long are DII, Client, Pro; net short are FII. Futures are a zero-sum market, so every net long is matched by a net short elsewhere. The informative question is which side is the stronger hand: when foreign institutions and Pro are short while Client is long, the weaker hands hold the long side and a fall tends to be sharper; when FIIs are long against a short retail crowd, a rise tends to be better supported.

Dollar Index fell 0.09% to 103.81. A weaker dollar loosens global liquidity and tends to encourage flows into emerging markets, supporting the rupee and FII buying. It is a tailwind for Indian equities, especially if it persists for several sessions.

Brent crude rose 0.64% to 92.57. India imports most of its oil, so higher crude widens the import bill and the current-account deficit, weakens the rupee, adds to inflation and squeezes margins of oil marketers, paint makers, airlines and chemicals. It is a headwind for the index; a sharp sustained rise raises the risk of a rate-cut delay.

India VIX rose 7.51% to 17.32. This is a normal level, consistent with an orderly market. A rising VIX with a falling index signals fear; a falling VIX with a rising index signals a comfortable uptrend, while a rising VIX with a rising index suggests traders are buying protection against the rally ending.

USD/INR fell 0.67% to 92.67. A firmer rupee tends to accompany foreign inflows and eases inflation, a tailwind for the broader market, though it trims exporters earnings.
